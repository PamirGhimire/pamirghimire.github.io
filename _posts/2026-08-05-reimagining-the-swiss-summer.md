Last Sunday, I stood near Basel's main bridge across the Rhine and looked at the clock outside a local Apotheke. Beneath the time, it showed the temperature: **38.5 °C**.

Growing up in Nepal, I would never have imagined a Swiss summer feeling like that. Switzerland belonged, in my mind, to a cooler geography: a place where summer meant going outside, enjoying the sun, hiking through forests or in the Alps, playing sports, and sitting beside a river. Heat was part of summer's pleasure—not something that made the outdoors feel hostile.

That Sunday in Basel, the heat was honestly unbearable.

The 38.5 °C display was a street-level reading, not an official meteorological measurement. At the MeteoSwiss station in Basel/Binningen, the official daily maximum on 2 August was **34.6 °C**. The difference is a useful reminder that the temperature we experience in a city can vary from the standardized measurement at a weather station. Sun-warmed stone, asphalt, walls, traffic, and limited airflow shape the heat around an individual street. Both numbers describe something real, but they describe it differently.

## The summer in the data

A single hot Sunday can still be dismissed as weather. The longer record makes it harder to dismiss the direction in which Basel's summers are moving.

![Average summer temperature in Basel/Binningen from 1755 to 2025]({{ '/assets/posts/reimagining-swiss-summer/basel-summer-temperatures.svg' | relative_url }})

*Figure 1: Average June–August temperature at the Basel/Binningen station. The thin line shows individual summers; the red line is the 30-year moving average. Source: MeteoSwiss Open Data.*

The Basel/Binningen record begins in 1755. Individual summers fluctuate considerably, but the long-term curve rises sharply toward the present. The average summer temperature was **17.5 °C** during 1961–1990 and **19.4 °C** during 1991–2020. For the eleven summers from 2015 through 2025, it was **20.6 °C**.

That is a change large enough to affect not only records and averages, but ordinary expectations: when we go outside, what time of day we exercise, whether an apartment can cool down at night, and whether a sunny afternoon feels inviting or dangerous.

## A different Rhine

The Rhine also looked unfamiliar. The water seemed extraordinarily low. Algae and dried material marked the exposed banks and embankment walls, giving the river a depleted, unpleasant appearance—far removed from the Rhine I carried in my imagination.

The low water was not merely an impression. A few days after my visit, the Swiss Federal Office for the Environment recorded a 24-hour mean discharge of about **460 m³/s** at Basel's Rheinhalle station. The long-term August mean is **1,245 m³/s**, and the lowest historical daily August mean listed for the station is 461 m³/s, recorded in 2022. At the same time, Rhine levels farther downstream in Germany were reported near record lows after prolonged heat and scarce rainfall.

A river is never a fixed postcard, of course. Its level and appearance change with rain, snowmelt, season, and upstream conditions. Still, seeing the heat and the diminished river together made the abstract language of climate trends feel physical.

## What happens to the idea of summer?

For a long time, the European cultural image of summer has been uncomplicated: light, freedom, terraces, festivals, hiking, sport, and long days outdoors. That image appears in tourism, advertising, school holidays, and everyday conversation. We wait through winter for summer to arrive.

But what happens when the sun is no longer an invitation for significant parts of the day?

During a heatwave, the old summer routine stops working. A hike at midday becomes unwise. Sport moves to early morning or late evening. Sitting beside the river may offer little relief. Homes designed to retain warmth become difficult to cool. Public spaces without trees or shade become places to cross quickly rather than places to inhabit.

This does not mean that summer in Switzerland will cease to be joyful. It means that joy may need a different timetable and different infrastructure. Outdoor life shifts toward mornings and evenings. Shade, drinking water, cooler buildings, trees, and accessible indoor refuges become part of what makes a city livable. The ability to escape the heat also becomes a question of fairness: not everyone has air conditioning, a cool apartment, flexible working hours, or the option to leave the city.

Perhaps the cultural adjustment will be subtle at first. We may stop treating every clear, hot day as "beautiful weather." We may plan summer events around heat rather than merely rain. We may come to value shade as much as sunshine, and judge streets and buildings by how well they protect people in July and August.

The Switzerland I imagined as a child in Nepal still exists: the forests, mountains, rivers, long evenings, and pleasure of being outdoors are all real. But the 38.5 °C on that pharmacy clock was also real. So was the depleted Rhine in front of me.

Swiss summer now contains both pictures. Going forward, European culture may have to learn to hold them together.
